1. Overview

Why geometry-aware trajectory encoding matters

DHB-XR (Double-reflection Householder Bishop frame with eXtended Representation) encodes robot end-effector trajectories as sequences of discrete curvature and torsion invariants that are completely independent of where or how the trajectory is positioned in the workspace. The key insight is simple but powerful: a trajectory's shape is independent of where it is executed. A pouring motion is the same pouring motion whether the cup sits on the left side of the table or the right.

Concretely, given a sequence of $N$ Cartesian waypoints $\{\mathbf{p}_i\}_{i=0}^{N-1} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, DHB-XR produces a sequence of $N-2$ invariant 4-tuples $(m_i, \varphi_x, \varphi_y, \varphi_z)$ that encode the magnitude of each step and the frame rotation between consecutive steps. Applying any rigid-body transform $T \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$ to the input trajectory produces exactly the same invariants, bit-for-bit identical in double precision.

This property connects directly to the fundamental theorem of space curves in differential geometry: curvature $\kappa(s)$ and torsion $\tau(s)$ determine a curve uniquely up to rigid motion. DHB invariants are the discrete, numerically robust analogue of $(\kappa, \tau)$, computed via Householder reflections rather than cross products, which makes them singularity-free even at inflection points where the classical Frenet-Serret frame degenerates.

2. Moving Frames on Curves

From Frenet-Serret to rotation-minimizing Bishop frames

2.1 Frenet-Serret Frame

The classical approach associates a moving orthonormal frame $\{\mathbf{T}, \mathbf{N}, \mathbf{B}\}$ to each point on a smooth curve $\gamma(s)$ parameterized by arc length:

$$ \mathbf{T} = \gamma'(s), \quad \mathbf{N} = \frac{\mathbf{T}'}{\|\mathbf{T}'\|}, \quad \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{T} \times \mathbf{N} $$

The evolution of this frame is governed by the Frenet-Serret equations:

$$ \frac{d}{ds} \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{T} \\ \mathbf{N} \\ \mathbf{B} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \kappa & 0 \\ -\kappa & 0 & \tau \\ 0 & -\tau & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{T} \\ \mathbf{N} \\ \mathbf{B} \end{pmatrix} $$
Problem: Inflection-Point Singularity

When $\kappa(s) = 0$ (the curve has an inflection point or is locally straight), the normal $\mathbf{N}$ is undefined because $\mathbf{T}' = \mathbf{0}$. This is not a rare edge case in robotics: straight-line segments between waypoints are extremely common.

2.2 Bishop Frame (Rotation-Minimizing Frame)

The Bishop frame $\{\mathbf{T}, \mathbf{U}_1, \mathbf{U}_2\}$ avoids this singularity by choosing normal vectors that undergo zero rotation about the tangent (rotation-minimizing). Instead of curvature $\kappa$ and torsion $\tau$, the Bishop frame uses two curvature components $k_1, k_2$ related by:

$$ \kappa = \sqrt{k_1^2 + k_2^2}, \qquad \theta(s) = \arctan\!\left(\frac{k_2}{k_1}\right), \qquad \tau = \frac{d\theta}{ds} $$

The frame evolves smoothly even through inflection points because neither $k_1$ nor $k_2$ appears in a denominator.

2.3 DHB-DR: Double-Reflection Frame Update

For a discrete polyline $\{\mathbf{p}_i\}$, we need a way to propagate the Bishop frame from step $i$ to step $i+1$ without computing cross products. DHB uses the double-reflection method of Bloomenthal and Lim (described by Wang et al. 2008), which propagates an exact rotation-minimizing frame via two Householder reflections.

Definition: DHB-DR Frame Propagation

Let $F_i = [\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{y}_i, \mathbf{z}_i]$ be the Bishop frame at step $i$, and let $\Delta\mathbf{d}_i = \mathbf{p}_{i+1} - \mathbf{p}_i$ be the chord vector. Define:

$$ \mathbf{v}_1 = \Delta\mathbf{d}_{i+1} - \Delta\mathbf{d}_i, \qquad \mathbf{u}_1 = \frac{\mathbf{v}_1}{\|\mathbf{v}_1\|} $$

First reflection (aligns old tangent toward new tangent):

$$ R_1 = I - 2\,\mathbf{u}_1 \mathbf{u}_1^\top, \qquad \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ref}} = R_1 \, \mathbf{x}_i $$

Second reflection (corrects the remaining tilt):

$$ \mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{x}_{i+1} - \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ref}}, \qquad \mathbf{u}_2 = \frac{\mathbf{v}_2}{\|\mathbf{v}_2\|} $$ $$ \mathbf{y}_{i+1} = \left(I - 2\,\mathbf{u}_2 \mathbf{u}_2^\top\right) \left(I - 2\,\mathbf{u}_1 \mathbf{u}_1^\top\right) \mathbf{y}_i $$

where $\mathbf{x}_{i+1} = \Delta\mathbf{d}_{i+1} / \|\Delta\mathbf{d}_{i+1}\|$ is the new tangent and $\mathbf{z}_{i+1} = \mathbf{x}_{i+1} \times \mathbf{y}_{i+1}$.

Each reflection is a Householder transformation, which is orthogonal by construction. The composition of two Householder reflections is a proper rotation (determinant $+1$), ensuring the frame stays right-handed and unit-orthonormal to machine precision.

3. Invariant Encoding

From frames to SE(3)-invariant tokens

Given the Bishop frame sequence $\{F_i\}$, we extract two types of invariants at each step:

3.1 Magnitude Invariant

The step magnitude is the projection of the displacement onto the tangent direction:

$$ m_i = \mathbf{x}_i \cdot \Delta\mathbf{p}_i = \|\Delta\mathbf{p}_i\| $$

Since $\mathbf{x}_i$ is the unit tangent aligned with $\Delta\mathbf{p}_i$, the magnitude is simply the Euclidean step length, which is invariant under rigid transforms.

3.2 Frame Rotation Invariants

The relative rotation between consecutive frames captures how the curve bends and twists:

$$ \Delta R_i = F_i^\top F_{i+1} \in \mathrm{SO}(3) $$

We extract three scalar angles from $\Delta R_i$ via the Euler XYZ decomposition:

$$ (\varphi_x, \varphi_y, \varphi_z) = \mathrm{EulerXYZ}(\Delta R_i) $$
Definition: DHB Invariant Tuple

The invariant encoding at step $i$ is the 4-tuple:

$$ \mathbf{h}_i = (m_i,\; \varphi_x^{(i)},\; \varphi_y^{(i)},\; \varphi_z^{(i)}) \in \mathbb{R}^4 $$

A trajectory of $N$ waypoints yields $N-2$ invariant tuples (the first two points define the initial frame and are consumed).

Theorem: SE(3)-Invariance

For any rigid-body transform $T = (R, \mathbf{t}) \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$ and trajectory $\{\mathbf{p}_i\}$, the DHB invariants of $\{R\mathbf{p}_i + \mathbf{t}\}$ are identical to those of $\{\mathbf{p}_i\}$.

Proof sketch. Translation: $\Delta\mathbf{p}_i' = R\mathbf{p}_{i+1} + \mathbf{t} - R\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{t} = R\,\Delta\mathbf{p}_i$. The magnitude $\|R\,\Delta\mathbf{p}_i\| = \|\Delta\mathbf{p}_i\|$ since $R$ is orthogonal. Rotation: The transformed Bishop frame is $F_i' = R\,F_i$. Thus $\Delta R_i' = (RF_i)^\top (RF_{i+1}) = F_i^\top R^\top R\,F_{i+1} = F_i^\top F_{i+1} = \Delta R_i$. Both components are exactly preserved. $\square$

3.3 DHB-QR Variant: Quaternion Representation

The Euler angle decomposition $(\varphi_x, \varphi_y, \varphi_z)$ suffers from gimbal lock when $\varphi_y \to \pm\pi/2$. The DHB-QR variant instead stores the unit quaternion $\mathbf{q}_i \in \mathbb{S}^3$ representing $\Delta R_i$:

$$ \mathbf{h}_i^{\mathrm{QR}} = (m_i,\; q_w,\; q_x,\; q_y,\; q_z) \in \mathbb{R}^5 $$

This adds one dimension per step but avoids all singularities. For VLA tokenization, the Euler variant is preferred (fewer tokens), since the inter-frame rotations are typically small and far from gimbal lock.

3.4 DHB-TI: Arc-Length Reparameterization

The magnitudes $\{m_i\}$ encode the absolute step sizes, which change if the trajectory is executed at a different speed. For speed-invariant encoding, DHB-TI normalizes each magnitude by the total arc length $L = \sum_i m_i$:

$$ \hat{m}_i = \frac{m_i}{L}, \qquad \sum_i \hat{m}_i = 1 $$

The result is a Sim(3)-invariant representation: invariant under translation, rotation, and uniform scaling.

4. Decoding & Adaptation

Reconstructing and adapting trajectories from invariants

4.1 Forward Integration

Given an initial position $\mathbf{p}_0$, a second position $\mathbf{p}_1$ (which together define the initial tangent $\mathbf{x}_0$), and an initial frame $F_0$, the trajectory is reconstructed by iterating:

$$ F_{i+1} = F_i \cdot \mathrm{RotXYZ}(\varphi_x^{(i)}, \varphi_y^{(i)}, \varphi_z^{(i)}) $$ $$ \mathbf{p}_{i+2} = \mathbf{p}_{i+1} + m_i \cdot \mathbf{x}_{i+1} $$

where $\mathbf{x}_{i+1}$ is the first column of $F_{i+1}$ (the tangent vector).

Exact Invertibility

The encode-decode round-trip is exact in IEEE 754 double precision: $\mathrm{Decode}(\mathrm{Encode}(\{\mathbf{p}_i\}), \mathbf{p}_0, \mathbf{p}_1, F_0) = \{\mathbf{p}_i\}$ with MSE $\approx 10^{-33}$. This is a consequence of the Householder reflection structure: each reflection is involutory ($R^2 = I$), so the forward and inverse operations cancel to machine epsilon.

4.2 Adaptation via Initial Conditions

Since the invariants are SE(3)-independent, adapting a trajectory to a new start pose is as simple as decoding with different initial conditions. For a pure translation offset $\delta \in \mathbb{R}^3$:

$$ \mathbf{p}_0' = \mathbf{p}_0 + \delta, \quad \mathbf{p}_1' = \mathbf{p}_1 + \delta, \quad F_0' = F_0 $$

This is a closed-form solution requiring zero optimization. The decoded trajectory is an exact rigid translation of the original.

4.3 Constrained Adaptation via NLP

For more complex adaptations (obstacle avoidance, via-point constraints, orientation targets), we formulate a nonlinear program (NLP) over the initial conditions:

$$ \min_{\mathbf{p}_0', \mathbf{p}_1', F_0'} \; \sum_i \|\mathbf{h}_i' - \mathbf{h}_i\|^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad g(\{\mathbf{p}_j'\}) \leq 0 $$

where $g$ encodes obstacle avoidance or workspace constraints. Since the invariants are smooth functions of the initial conditions, this NLP is well-conditioned and solvable with standard tools (Fatrop, CasADi, IPOPT).

6. Full 7-DOF Invariance

Position + orientation + gripper in a unified invariant representation

Robot manipulation trajectories are not just 3D curves. Each waypoint carries a full 6-DOF pose (position + orientation) plus a scalar gripper command, totaling 7 DOF. DHB-XR handles each component:

6.1 Position Invariants (3-DOF)

The 4-tuple $(m_i, \varphi_x, \varphi_y, \varphi_z)$ described above, invariant under SE(3) applied to the position coordinates.

6.2 Orientation Invariants (3-DOF)

End-effector orientations $\{R_i^{\mathrm{ee}}\} \subset \mathrm{SO}(3)$ are encoded as relative rotations in the Bishop frame:

$$ \Delta R_i^{\mathrm{ee}} = (F_i^\top R_i^{\mathrm{ee}})^\top (F_{i+1}^\top R_{i+1}^{\mathrm{ee}}) $$

Expressing the end-effector orientation relative to the Bishop frame factors out the trajectory's own rotation, yielding three additional invariant angles per step.

6.3 Gripper Encoding (1-DOF)

The scalar gripper state $g_i \in [0, 1]$ is re-parameterized by arc-length fraction:

$$ \hat{g}(s) = g\!\left(\frac{s}{L}\right), \qquad s = \sum_{j=0}^{i} m_j $$

This binds the gripper state to the geometric progress along the curve rather than to a time index, making it invariant under speed changes and spatial transforms.

Combined Invariant Vector

The full 7-DOF invariant at each step is a 8-dimensional vector:

$$ \mathbf{h}_i^{\text{full}} = \left(m_i,\; \varphi_x,\; \varphi_y,\; \varphi_z,\; \psi_x,\; \psi_y,\; \psi_z,\; \hat{g}_i\right) \in \mathbb{R}^8 $$

where $(\psi_x, \psi_y, \psi_z)$ are the orientation invariant angles.

7. Connections to Differential Geometry

DHB invariants as discrete curvature and torsion

7.1 The Fundamental Theorem of Space Curves

Theorem (Classical)

A smooth regular curve $\gamma: [0, L] \to \mathbb{R}^3$ is uniquely determined, up to rigid motion, by its curvature function $\kappa(s) > 0$ and torsion function $\tau(s)$.

This is the continuous analogue of what DHB achieves discretely. The DHB invariants $(\varphi_x, \varphi_y, \varphi_z)$ encode the same information as $(\kappa, \tau)$ but in a representation that works for polylines (discrete curves) without requiring smoothness or non-vanishing curvature.

7.2 Discrete Curvature-Torsion Correspondence

For a discrete polyline, the classical discrete curvature at vertex $i$ is the turning angle $\theta_i = \arccos(\mathbf{x}_i \cdot \mathbf{x}_{i+1})$. The DHB Euler angles $(\varphi_x, \varphi_y, \varphi_z)$ decompose this turning into three orthogonal components relative to the rotation-minimizing frame:

$$ \theta_i = \|\mathrm{Log}(\Delta R_i)\|, \qquad \kappa_i \approx \frac{\theta_i}{(m_i + m_{i+1})/2} $$

The torsion is encoded in the $\varphi_z$ component (rotation about the tangent axis), while $\varphi_x$ and $\varphi_y$ encode the two components of curvature in the Bishop normal plane.

7.3 Gauge-Theoretic Interpretation

The choice of initial frame $F_0$ is a gauge freedom. Different choices of $F_0$ produce different frame sequences $\{F_i\}$ but identical relative rotations $\Delta R_i = F_i^\top F_{i+1}$. In the language of fiber bundles, the Bishop frame is a section of the frame bundle $\mathrm{SO}(3) \to \mathcal{F}(\gamma) \to \gamma$, and the invariants $\{\Delta R_i\}$ are the discrete connection (holonomy data).

The SE(3)-invariance of DHB is then the statement that the discrete connection is independent of the base-point gauge: applying $T \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$ changes the section ($F_i \mapsto R \cdot F_i$) but not the connection ($\Delta R_i$ is unchanged).

8. Numerical Properties

Why the round-trip error is astronomically small

8.1 Why MSE $\approx 10^{-33}$

The Householder reflection $R = I - 2\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^\top$ is involutory: $R^2 = I$ exactly in floating point (no rounding). When the encode step computes $R_1, R_2$ and the decode step applies $R_2, R_1$ in reverse, the cancellation is exact to the last bit. The residual $\sim 10^{-33}$ comes only from the Euler angle extraction and re-composition, which introduces $O(\epsilon^2)$ error where $\epsilon \approx 10^{-16}$ is machine epsilon.

8.2 Edge Cases

Collinear Steps

When three consecutive waypoints are collinear, $\mathbf{v}_1 = \Delta\mathbf{d}_{i+1} - \Delta\mathbf{d}_i$ is a zero vector, and the first reflection is undefined. DHB handles this by detecting $\|\mathbf{v}_1\| < \epsilon$ and setting $R_1 = I$ (no reflection needed, since the tangent direction has not changed). The frame propagates unchanged through straight segments.

Zero Velocity

If $\Delta\mathbf{p}_i = \mathbf{0}$ (the robot pauses at a waypoint), the tangent is undefined. DHB-XR handles this by carrying the previous tangent forward: $\mathbf{x}_{i+1} = \mathbf{x}_i$, $m_i = 0$. The frame rotation is recorded as identity.

180-degree Rotations

When the tangent reverses direction ($\mathbf{x}_{i+1} = -\mathbf{x}_i$), the Householder reflection axis lies in the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf{x}_i$. The double-reflection algorithm handles this correctly because Householder reflections are well-defined for any non-zero vector. The Euler angles will show $\varphi_y = \pm\pi$, which is representable (though near gimbal lock for the Euler variant; the quaternion variant DHB-QR avoids this).
